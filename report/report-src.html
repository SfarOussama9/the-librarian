1. The corpus and its users

Users. Tunisian students (from the baccalauréat to the PhD) preparing to study in France, Germany, Canada or in an Erasmus+ programme. Their questions have precise, checkable answers: deadlines, fees, required amounts, documents, eligibility and work rights. A wrong answer, such as an outdated blocked-account amount, can cost someone a visa.

Corpus. 281 official documents (266 web pages, 15 PDFs, ≈251,000 words, about 500 pages) from 11 publishers: Campus France Tunisie, Campus France, France-Visas, DAAD and its 88-programme scholarship database, DAAD Tunisie, the German embassy in Tunis, the Federal Foreign Office, IRCC, EduCanada, the European Commission and the Erasmus+ office in Tunisia. It is in French and English, all public, and contains no personal data. Every document is listed in corpus/sources.toml and recorded in a manifest (URL, date, method, checksum).

Evaluation set. 125 questions (58 in French, 67 in English): 110 answerable and 15 that should get "I don't know". 27 are cross-lingual, and some involve conflicting or outdated sources. Each stores its answer and an exact quote from the source document, which a script checks against the documents. All were reviewed by the team, and 26 of the hardest were checked against the original pages. We split them into dev (90) and test (35); chunking and retrieval settings were tuned on dev.

2. Pipeline design

Fetch + clean
per-site selectors → Markdown› Chunk
150 words, 25 overlap› BM25 + e5 vectors
top 50 each› RRF fusion
k = 60› Cross-encoder
reranks top 20› Top 4 chunks
≈500 words› gpt-5.4-nano
JSON: answer, cited
  • Cleaning. One content selector per publisher keeps headings, lists and tables as Markdown. PDFs are read page by page with running headers removed. Publication dates are extracted for 80 documents and used to prefer newer sources.
  • Chunking. Units (heading, paragraph, list, table) are packed into ≈150-word chunks. Headings are never left at the end of a chunk, and tables are split between rows with their header repeated. Each chunk keeps its URL, title, heading path, country, topic, language, date and PDF page. The size was tested (150 vs 250 vs 400 words at equal context): 150 gave the best recall (0.886 vs 0.873 vs 0.823 at 500 words).
  • Index. BM25 with a French/English tokenizer that folds accents, plus multilingual-e5-small vectors in a NumPy file. 2,200 vectors are searched exactly in under 10 ms; a vector database would add a server for no gain at this size.
  • Retrieval. Hybrid search (Reciprocal Rank Fusion), metadata filters (country, topic, language), then bge-reranker-v2-m3 (int8) on the top 20. A depth of 10 was 2× faster but lost 7.6 points of recall@3.
  • Generation. The model answers only from the numbered sources, puts [S#] after every factual sentence, and answers in the question's language. For the same value in the same situation the newest dated source wins and the older figure is mentioned. Structured output (answer, answerable, sources_used) makes "I don't know" a scorable flag and maps each citation to a URL and page.
  • Service. FastAPI with a streamed web front end, deployed on Modal (4 vCPU, scales to zero). It has a cache for repeated questions, per-visitor rate limits and anonymous logs.

  • Most misses are cross-lingual: a French question whose answer is only on an English page (IRCC, DAAD).

4. Generation results

Every answer was graded by an LLM judge (gpt-5.4-mini) against the gold answer and the chunks the model saw. Accuracy counts a correct answer (a partially correct one scores 0.5) or a correct "I don't know". The table gives the mean of 3 runs on all 125 questions. One run varies by up to 3 points.

MetricWeek 7 (gpt-5.4-mini)Final (gpt-5.4-nano)
Accuracy (all 125 questions)92.7%90.8%
Faithfulness (every claim supported by a cited chunk)96.8%96.2%
Citation accuracy (the cited chunk says it)95.5%94.6%
"I don't know" on unanswerable questions100%97.8%
Wrong "I don't know" on answerable questions6.4%5.5%

Judge check. We compared 15 random verdicts against the answers and their sources: 14 of 15 agree. The one error was lenient (q014), so the accuracy numbers are, if anything, slightly optimistic.

What fails. Wrong "I don't know" answers all come from the gold chunk not being retrieved, mostly in cross-lingual cases. Two rule fixes came from reading failures. Sources describing different situations (language course vs degree) are not conflicts, and a language hint plus a retry stop the model from switching language or script.

5. Week 8: what we changed and what it saved

Search results were computed once and saved, so every setup answers from the same chunks. Each setup ran 3 times on the same 125 questions.

Result: cost per question −69% ($0.00155 → $0.00048) for −1.9 accuracy points (92.7% → 90.8%; ranges 92.0–93.2 vs 90.4–91.2). p95 latency is unchanged (7.0 s → 7.6 s on a laptop).
  • Smaller model + more reasoning. Nano at low effort misreads numbers (−5.9 points). Its output tokens are 3.6× cheaper, so high effort recovers most of the loss and stays cheap.
  • Newest source first gives the same accuracy but brings faithfulness and citations back near the Week 7 level.
  • Prompt caching: no saving. Our fixed prompt (385 tokens) is under OpenAI's 1,024-token minimum. Padded to 1,156 tokens, mini cached it but nano did not, and the examples lowered accuracy.
  • Repeated questions are answered from a cache in the service, with no search and no model call.
  • Latency −50% was not reachable. The answer step alone takes 3.4 s at p95. Faster rerankers lost too much recall (MiniLM: −16 points, English only); jina-v2 cut search p95 by 22%, and skipping the reranker when BM25 and vector search agree skipped 30% of questions without moving p95.
  • Regression: nano answers the German blocked-account question in French (q043) with an outdated figure in all 3 runs, while mini was always right.
